# Settled, or merely proposed

Warrant asks *how do you know this*. Status asks *how settled is this in the field*. They are
different axes, and a claim can be perfectly warranted and barely established -- which is the
dangerous cell, and the one this exists to keep visible.

| status | what earns it | how it may be written |
|---|---|---|
| `textbook` | graduate texts treat it as background | bare assertion |
| `established` | independent replication, disjoint author sets | bare assertion, review cited |
| `contested` | serious work on both sides, both logged | **must name both sides** |
| `emerging` | one or two groups, recent | "early evidence suggests", groups counted |
| `single-result` | one paper, one setting | **attributed and situated, never bare** |
| `folklore` | widely repeated, trail never reaches data | marked unsourced, or dropped |

The failure that costs years is not citing something false. It is citing something **true but
unsettled** as though it were settled, and building on top of it.

Status is **capped by source independence**, computed from author identifiers rather than
declared. Two papers sharing an author cannot produce `established`.

Pointed the other way, the same axis protects an open question from being closed on the
user's behalf. A live disagreement is `contested`, and saying so keeps a door open that a
confident summary would quietly shut. When the evidence genuinely is thin, thin is the
finding -- not a gap to paper over with a more assured sentence.
